# Hidden Assumptions

* Spiritual phenomena can be reduced entirely to material processes.  
* Repetition of a belief directly rewires reality to match the belief.  
* Miracles, visions, and extraordinary experiences are solely products of mental conditioning (placebo/bias).  
* The mind/self is a singular substrate‑independent entity that can be “chewed” like food.  
* The “Still” and “Loud” represent two distinct parts of a unified self without empirical justification.  
* The author’s materialist stance negates any transcendent or divine influence.  
* The digestion metaphor fully captures how concepts integrate into the self.  
* Motivated reasoning explains all “miraculous timing” of outcomes.  
* The claim that “we are God” is a literal self‑identification rather than a metaphorical statement.  
* The process of learning always involves a binary “chew‑or‑swallow” decision.

# Weak Points

* The argument leaps from materialist premises to grand conclusions without intermediate steps.  
* Key terms such as “spiritual as a function of the physical” are never clearly defined, leading to ambiguity.  
* The digestion model of learning is metaphorical but is presented as a mechanistic explanation, blurring analogy and theory.  
* Citations or evidence for claims about placebo effects, neurobiology, or Buddhist philosophy are absent.  
* The “Still” vs. “Loud” dichotomy is introduced without criteria for distinguishing them, making it speculative.  
* The note conflates personal belief (“telling yourself something”) with objective scientific explanation.  
* Logical gaps appear when linking mental conditioning to external events (e.g., miracles, divine timing).  
* The assertion that “everything is subjective” is presented alongside materialist claims, creating an internal tension.

# Unanswered Questions

* In what concrete way does the physical substrate generate what we label “spiritual”?  
* How does the proposed digestion model map onto known cognitive processes like schema formation or memory consolidation?  
* What mechanisms could explain “miracles” beyond the placebo effect, especially when they involve observable, measurable outcomes?  
* How does the “Still” part of the self interact with the “Loud” part, and can this be empirically investigated?  
* Does the model account for both helpful and harmful conditioning, and what determines the direction of influence?  
* How does the claim that “we are God” inform ethical or practical behavior within this framework?  
* What role does the body’s neurochemistry play in the “chewing” and “synthesizing” stages of learning?  
* Can the digestion metaphor be operationalized into experimental paradigms or educational strategies?  
* How does the author reconcile the materialist stance with the reported experiences of visions and epiphanies?  
* What evidence exists that motivated reasoning alone can create “miraculous timing” in real-world outcomes?

# Overall Assessment

The note is bold in ambition, attempting to collapse the transcendent into a materialist framework while offering a vivid metaphor for learning. This willingness to tackle big philosophical questions and to tie them to concrete cognitive processes is a strong point and shows creative thinking. However, the analysis suffers from a lack of clear definitions, evidential support, and consistent reasoning—key weaknesses that leave many claims in the realm of speculation.

The next step is toground the model in empirical research: delineate how “chewing” maps onto conceptual analysis, test the “still vs. loud” distinction through phenomenological or neuroimaging studies, and examine whether the proposed mechanisms can account for placebo‑type phenomena and reported miracles without resorting to unverified assumptions. Clarifying these gaps will transform the note from a provocative essay into a testable hypothesis.
